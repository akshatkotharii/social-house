# Social House — Expert Feedback & Iterations

> Living creative-direction record. Use this before ideation, storyboarding, design, generation, editing, and performance review.

## Core principle

Do not make isolated "pretty posts." Build a recognisable world that compounds across the whole page.

For a textile brand, the product must feel physically present: viewers should almost feel the weave, embroidery, weight, drape, and light through the screen.

## 1. Decide the brand feel before the content format

Before choosing a Reel, carousel, voice, trend, shot, or caption, define what the brand should feel like. This choice informs every downstream decision.

| Brand feel | Visual language | Motion and edit | Type and composition |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Luxury | Muted, restrained palette; material close-ups; refined light | Slow, deliberate movement; longer holds | Elegant serif with minimal supporting sans; generous negative space |
| Modern premium | Clean editorial imagery; precise product framing | Sharp, considered cuts; no visual clutter | Minimal sans or restrained serif/sans pairing; clear grid |
| Traditional | Warm tones; cultural cues; detailed motifs and craft | Softer transitions; patient material reveals | Heritage-inspired serif or graceful display type; decorative detail used sparingly |
| Young / trendy | Contemporary styling; bold but controlled colour | Faster rhythm, current formats, playful but purposeful transitions | Contemporary type, short hooks, high clarity |
| Artisanal | Imperfect beauty; hands, processes, provenance | Observational, tactile, human pacing | Quiet typography; craft details outweigh graphic treatment |
| Minimal | One strong visual idea; monochrome or limited palette | Few cuts; intentional stillness | One type family or disciplined pairing; extreme restraint |

### Required early decision

Every project must have a one-line **Brand Feel Statement** before concepts are generated.

Example: *"Quiet Indian luxury: heirloom craft shown with contemporary editorial restraint."*

If a Reel could belong to any brand after its logo is removed, it has not passed this step.

## 2. Make the fabric the hero

Avoid generic claims such as "beautiful cotton fabric." Demonstrate why it is beautiful.

### Tactile shot library

- Extreme macro of weave, embroidery, thread, zari, print, motif, edge, and stitching.
- Fabric moving slowly in air or across a surface.
- Fingers passing over the texture; hands folding, lifting, draping, and comparing.
- Daylight and controlled directional light travelling across the surface.
- Full fabric fall and weight; edge/border reveal; layered folds.
- Material beside a finished garment or on a real wearer, if product truth allows it.
- One proof shot showing the actual product cleanly and without visual manipulation.

### Rule

Show texture before describing texture. The image, sound, and movement should carry the claim.

For generated visuals, never let AI invent or alter the actual border, motif, threadwork, colour, or product characteristics. Use real product footage for proof.

## 3. Hooks do not need words

The first second may be a visual or sensory event rather than text.

Strong non-text hooks:

- A border unfolding into frame.
- A macro detail resolving into the whole fabric.
- Light catching embroidery.
- A sudden hand movement, drape, or fabric drop.
- A striking colour or tactile close-up.
- A before/after garment transformation, only when accurate.

Text earns its place only when it adds meaning, intrigue, clarity, or a reason to act.

## 4. Typography must compound

Standardise a small type system at brand level:

- One primary font.
- One supporting font.
- For premium brands, usually a serif + sans-serif pairing.
- Defined roles: headline, product detail, price/offer, CTA, and captions.
- Defined size range, weights, line-height, case, colour, and safe-zone rules.

More than two families normally lowers perceived value. Typography should feel authored, not template-driven.

## 5. Negative space signals confidence

Do not fill every empty area with text, stickers, animation, or graphics.

- Let the fabric breathe.
- Use one small line of copy when one line is enough.
- Hold a beautiful product frame long enough to be seen.
- Keep margins, caption placement, and graphic density consistent.

Premium content often says less, with more control.

## 6. Go beyond the brief

For each brand, ask: **What useful extra can we create that was not explicitly paid for, but makes the brand feel deeply understood?**

Examples:

- A reusable tactile-shot map for every SKU.
- A seasonal visual moodboard and material palette.
- A recurring Reel opening/closing language.
- A dealer FAQ content series derived from common DMs.
- A product-detail carousel that a retailer can save and forward.
- A showroom shot list for staff to capture on a phone.
- A light, type, and composition guide that prevents future content drift.

The goal is to make the client feel there is a real content house behind the work, not a prompt machine producing files.

## 7. Treat cover and profile grid as separate design systems

A strong Reel can be ignored when its cover looks weak in the profile grid. Create the cover deliberately; never accept a random video frame as the default.

### Cover system

- Keep headline and logo placement consistent across covers.
- Make the cover communicate one clear reason to tap.
- Preserve material detail, readable hierarchy, and enough negative space at profile-thumbnail size.
- Use a recurring visual signature, but do not turn every cover into the same template.

### Grid system

Judge the last 12–15 posts together, not only one by one. The profile grid is an editorial layout with its own rhythm.

- Alternate image-heavy and text-heavy posts.
- Alternate macro, product-wide, portrait/model, process, and environment frames.
- Alternate visual intensity: detailed/busy posts need calmer neighbours.
- Keep headline/logo placements coherent enough to feel like one brand.
- Use content variety to prevent the grid becoming visually repetitive.

## 8. Compose with curiosity, asymmetry, and reveal

- Do not centre the important fabric feature by default. Intentional asymmetry can feel more editorial.
- Crop with purpose. Showing 30% of a product can create more curiosity than showing it all.
- Use ambiguous macro embroidery or texture as a visual mystery, then reveal full product later.
- For luxury, do not show the entire product immediately every time.
- Build `hide → tease → reveal` arcs across a Reel, carousel, or launch sequence.
- Use one recognisable scale cue when macro views need context.

## 9. Direct motion and edit continuity

Start with movement already underway when possible: fabric falling, a hand entering, a pull, a curtain opening, a turn, or a camera move. This avoids slow static starts.

- Match motion direction across cuts when it serves flow.
- Use eyelines: a model looking left can motivate a cut to something on that side.
- Use match cuts for textiles: same colour, fold, circular motif, thread line, or shape carried into next scene.
- Transition through colour palettes from one collection colour to another.
- Capture empty frames, lens covers, fabric passes, walk-bys, close-up movement, and flares as deliberate transition material.
- Record several seconds before and after each action; editing needs handles.

## 10. Create a subtle brand signature

Build one quiet, repeatable cue people gradually recognise without consciously noticing.

Possible signatures:

- A distinctive crop or macro treatment.
- A consistent text lockup or headline position.
- A recurring close-up, camera move, or transition language.
- A restrained sound motif.
- A particular colour, light treatment, or end frame.

Signature must add recognition, not gimmickry.

## 11. Separate visual movement from text movement

- Slow visuals with fast text can create tension.
- Fast visuals with slow text can create contrast.
- Never make typography compete with beautiful fabric movement.
- When material movement is strong, text stays simple.
- When imagery is static, typography can carry more movement.
- A still frame with excellent type can be more powerful than another transition.

## 12. Copy, captions, search, accessibility

### Caption hierarchy

- Opening line must earn the "more" tap: thought, question, observation, or small story.
- Never default to "Introducing our latest collection…".
- Use short paragraphs and deliberate line breaks.
- Write a brand caption guide: voice, punctuation, capitalisation, emoji policy, use of "we", and audience address.
- Premium brands use emojis sparingly or not at all, depending on personality.
- Hashtags are distribution support, never creative strategy.

### Search and access

- Naturally include relevant search language: fabric type, textile, handloom, embroidery, technique, colour, collection, city, and use case.
- Do not stuff keywords or make copy robotic.
- Provide useful alt text for information-led carousels.
- Design subtitles into brand system. Keep them readable without blocking the product; do not default to giant white text in black boxes.
- Test every Reel with sound on and sound off. Both versions must carry meaning.

## 13. Voiceover direction

- Record or generate multiple deliveries for same script: intimate, confident, conversational.
- Choose delivery after listening against final visual pacing.
- Natural delivery beats sales-announcer energy unless campaign calls for it.
- Standardise brand-name pronunciation.
- Do not use voice to declare claims imagery can prove better.

## 14. Heritage can look contemporary

- Do not turn heritage into an "old" visual cliché.
- Photograph craft and traditional motifs with modern light, styling, and composition.
- Avoid overloading frames with obvious cultural symbols.
- One exact detail can communicate heritage more credibly than a themed set.
- Show reverse embroidery, internal stitching, raw material, unfinished work, and small human irregularities when truthful.
- Imperfection can signal craft; it must never look like poor production.

## 15. Environment must tell product story

Move beyond white studio backgrounds when environment adds a reason:

- architecture
- natural light
- old walls
- modern interiors
- gardens
- markets
- workshops
- showroom and packaging contexts

Background is never decoration alone. It should explain mood, provenance, use, scale, contrast, or audience aspiration.

## 16. Process and satisfying micro-moments

Use built-in transformation stories:

- raw thread → finished fabric
- plain fabric → embroidery
- sketch → final design
- loom → garment
- design detail → product-wide reveal

Capture sensory, satisfying actions:

- folding
- cutting
- pulling thread
- aligning patterns
- unrolling fabric
- opening packaging
- hand across material
- drape and fold release

Build a reusable library of these clips. They create satisfying Reels even before a complex narrative exists.

## 17. Shoot for reusable coverage

Shoot vertical first for Reels/Stories. Also capture some wider master frames with crop room for other formats.

### Minimum shoot checklist per collection

- 3 macro texture shots
- 3 full-product shots
- 3 movement shots
- 3 hand-interaction shots
- 3 process shots
- 3 wide environment shots
- 3 model shots, when relevant
- 3 transition shots
- 3 packaging or behind-the-scenes shots

Additional rules:

- Capture longer takes than final edit needs.
- Capture clean reusable versions without text or logos.
- Avoid returning with many versions of one angle and no visual range.
- Organise originals by collection, colour, fabric, campaign, orientation, and date.

## 18. Metadata and content templates power automation

Every asset/product should carry usable metadata:

- fabric type
- colour
- season
- collection
- target audience
- price segment
- brand mood
- campaign
- content pillar
- available visuals
- available voiceover
- music category

This lets the system choose suitable content rather than repeating one Reel pattern.

Maintain objective-specific templates:

- luxury product reveal
- educational Reel
- craftsmanship Reel
- founder/story Reel
- trend adaptation
- educational carousel
- editorial carousel
- launch sequence

Templates preserve useful structure; creative expression must still change.

## 19. Design launches as campaigns, not announcements

Do not post "NEW COLLECTION OUT NOW" without buildup.

Suggested arc:

`teaser → detail → colour → process → story → reveal → launch → styling/customer content`

Keep some information unrevealed before launch. Curiosity is part of campaign design.

After launch, keep extracting stories from same collection:

- how it was made
- why this colour
- styling ideas
- close-up details
- behind the shoot
- customer reaction
- designer inspiration

## 20. Community supplies content strategy

- Save recurring comments and DMs.
- Turn repeated questions into Reels/carousels: cotton, washing, technique, price, availability, styling, origin.
- Give UGC prompts, not only "tag us": show your styling, texture close-up, unboxing, before/after, or occasion use.
- Treat questions as audience evidence, not assumptions.

## 21. Use analytics to improve creative choices

Track creative purpose, not only post frequency.

- Drop at 2 seconds: hook may be weak.
- High completion but low clicks: CTA or product connection may be weak.
- High saves on education: make more useful education.
- High shares on emotion/story: inspect why people share it.
- Compare content types, not only individual winners:
  - craftsmanship Reels: average watch time
  - educational carousels: saves
  - editorial Reels: profile visits
  - product Reels: clicks and DMs

Do not chase empty reach. Smaller audience with real textile interest can outperform broad, irrelevant views.

Track objective-relevant signals: saves, shares, profile visits, follows, completion rate, average watch time, clicks, qualified DMs, and sales attribution where available.

Test one variable at a time where possible:

- same visual, different hook
- same hook, different opening shot
- same concept, different length

Changing hook, edit style, music, length, and visual style together hides what worked.

## 22. Build a cross-category swipe file

Collect references to understand principles, not copy outcomes.

Study:

- typography
- composition
- camera movement
- storytelling
- sound
- colour
- transitions
- covers

Look beyond textile competitors: fashion, architecture, magazines, beauty, jewellery, luxury hotels, Pinterest, print campaigns, packaging, store interiors, and photography books.

## 23. Assign every post one job

Not every post must be viral.

- beauty and desirability
- education
- selling
- trust
- community
- memorability

Strong content leaves one durable memory: a line, texture, sound, colour, image, or moment.

Create an unanswered question in viewer's mind, then answer it:

- How was that made?
- What does that feel like?
- Why does that look different?
- Where is that from?
- What happens next?

## 24. Prove premium; do not announce it

Avoid generic claims: "luxurious", "high quality", "finest craftsmanship", "unparalleled excellence".

- Show craft instead of saying "finely crafted."
- Show movement and texture instead of saying "soft."
- Create timeless visual language instead of saying "timeless."
- Use specific, human copy: "Woven slowly. Finished by hand."
- Treat words as visual matter: one word can be enough — `TEXTURE`, `ORIGIN`, `CRAFT`, `DETAIL`, `THREAD`, `MOVEMENT`.
- Leave interpretive space. Premium/editorial content can retain mystery rather than explain every emotion.

## Operating sequence

1. Confirm brand feel statement and audience emotion.
2. Identify the product truth: what must remain visually exact.
3. Select one content purpose: desire, education, trust, community, inquiry, or conversion.
4. Choose a tactile visual hook before writing an on-screen text hook.
5. Write the story around material, person, and moment — not feature dumping.
6. Apply the brand type system and negative-space rule.
7. Run product-truth and human-feel QA before publishing.
8. Review saves, shares, DMs, watch-through, and comments; retain what compounds brand identity.

## Pre-publish quality gate

- Does this unmistakably feel like this brand?
- Is the fabric the visual hero for most of the piece?
- Can a viewer sense texture, weight, or craft?
- Is the first second compelling without relying on loud text?
- Is every word, transition, and graphic necessary?
- Are there only one or two font families?
- Is there enough negative space?
- Are real product details accurate and clearly shown?
- Does it feel like a human editorial decision, rather than an AI template?
- Would someone save, share, DM, or remember it?
